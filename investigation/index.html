Geotechnical site investigation in Richmond, BC, is the foundational process of characterizing subsurface soil and groundwater conditions to inform safe, economical, and code-compliant design of structures and infrastructure. Located within the Fraser River delta, Richmond poses unique challenges due to its thick sequences of Holocene-age deltaic deposits, high groundwater table, and significant seismic vulnerability. A robust investigation program here must delineate the depth and consistency of soft, compressible silts and clays, identify loose, liquefiable sand layers, and map the depth to competent bearing strata, which can often exceed 40 metres. This category encompasses a suite of in-situ testing and sampling techniques that move beyond simple borehole drilling to provide quantitative engineering parameters essential for deep foundations, liquefaction assessment, and settlement analysis.

The Quaternary geology of Richmond is dominated by up to 300 metres of unconsolidated sediments overlying Pleistocene glacial deposits and Tertiary bedrock. The near-surface stratigraphy typically consists of a crust of desiccated silty clay overlying normally consolidated to slightly overconsolidated marine and estuarine silts, interbedded with discontinuous sand lenses and channel fill deposits. These fine-grained soils exhibit low shear strength, high compressibility, and secondary compression characteristics. Critically, the sand units, particularly those of the Fort Langley Formation, are often in a loose state and are susceptible to cyclic softening and flow liquefaction during a design seismic event. A meaningful investigation must therefore target these discrete layers with high-resolution profiling methods.

All site investigation work in Richmond is governed by the British Columbia Building Code (BCBC) 2024, which references the National Building Code of Canada (NBC) 2020. Subsurface investigation requirements are detailed in Section 4.2 of the BCBC and the accompanying structural commentaries, with seismic site classification per Table 4.1.8.4.A. Professional practice is guided by Engineers and Geoscientists BC (EGBC) Professional Practice Guidelines for Site Investigations. Specifically, for seismic design, the guidelines mandate sufficient field testing to reliably determine the Site Class, typically requiring an average shear-wave velocity (Vs30) measurement or a Standard Penetration Test (SPT) blow count profile to a depth of at least 30 metres. Liquefaction assessment must follow the framework established in the NBC commentary, demanding high-quality data from penetration tests.

Projects mandating such detailed investigations are diverse and high-stakes. Multi-storey residential and commercial towers on deep pile foundations require precise delineation of the dense glacial till or bedrock surface. Critical infrastructure like the George Massey Tunnel replacement and dike upgrades along the Fraser River demand rigorous seismic stability analyses. Even low-rise industrial and warehouse developments on raft or shallow footings require a thorough assessment of post-construction settlement and bearing capacity failure modes. Specialized in-situ tests are the primary tools for this work. The SPT (Standard Penetration Test) remains a staple for disturbed sampling and index strength correlation, while the CPT (Cone Penetration Test) provides a near-continuous profile of tip resistance, sleeve friction, and pore pressure for detailed stratigraphic interpretation and liquefaction evaluation. For assessing shallow conditions or inspecting existing footings, exploratory test pits offer a direct visual examination of the soil fabric.

Frequently asked questions

What depth of investigation is typically required for a seismic site classification in Richmond?

For seismic site classification in accordance with the BC Building Code, subsurface investigations must typically extend to a minimum depth of 30 metres below the finished ground surface. This depth is necessary to calculate the average shear-wave velocity (Vs30) or equivalent Standard Penetration Test (SPT) N60 values, ensuring that the deep, potentially liquefiable deltaic sands are adequately characterized.

How do Richmond's unique soil conditions influence the choice between a CPT and an SPT investigation?

Richmond's thick sequences of soft, sensitive silts and interbedded loose sands make the CPT (Cone Penetration Test) exceptionally valuable. The CPT provides a continuous, high-resolution profile without the sample disturbance inherent to SPT drilling, allowing for precise identification of thin liquefiable layers and subtle changes in soil behaviour that govern settlement and slope stability analyses in deltaic deposits.

What regulations govern the professional practice of geotechnical site investigations in British Columbia?

Geotechnical investigations are governed by the BC Building Code and the National Building Code of Canada for structural design parameters. Professional practice is strictly regulated by Engineers and Geoscientists BC (EGBC), whose Professional Practice Guidelines for Site Investigations mandate the standard of care, required documentation, and the ethical obligations of the field and reviewing engineers.

What are the primary geotechnical hazards a site investigation aims to mitigate in the Fraser River delta?

The primary hazards include seismic liquefaction of loose sand layers, which can cause lateral spreading and foundation failure, and significant post-construction settlement of thick compressible silts. A comprehensive investigation also assesses the potential for cyclic softening of sensitive clays and long-term creep settlement, all of which require careful quantification for resilient design.
